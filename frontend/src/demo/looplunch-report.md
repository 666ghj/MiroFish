## Observed State and Forecast Limits

**The recorded run ends with a conditional discussion, not a launch decision.** It stopped early after 102 actions—53 on Twitter and 49 on Reddit—with Twitter reaching round 37 and Reddit round 33 of 336 planned rounds. The runner was stopped with a stop request recorded; this was not a completed scenario or a reduced-budget run. Findings therefore describe the recorded portion only, not the eventual pricing decision or six-week pilot outcome.

**Evidence quality limits the interpretation.** Three searches returned graph facts and summaries of attributed statements, but did not supply verbatim agent posts with action identifiers or round-level provenance. The quotations below reproduce those retrieved summaries exactly; they are not authenticated transcripts of agents’ original wording. Graph fact counts should not be treated as additional actions or independent endorsements.

The retrieved evidence describes Maya Tan’s preference as conditional rather than an approval:

> “Maya Tan still prefers testing S$12 for LoopLunch trial accessibility, but requires paid demand checked by weekday, verified costs and written delivery arrangements before committing; S$14 and postponement remain alternatives, neither launch nor price is confirmed, and the decision is due on 23 October 2026.”

Daniel Koh’s alternative preference also retains postponement:

> “Daniel Koh continues to prefer S$14 for the fictional LoopLunch pilot and recommends postponement if reliability and responsibilities remain unresolved by 23 October 2026.”

These positions support a narrow finding: the recorded discussion contains conditional support for both prices and postponement if requirements remain unmet. It does not establish a winning price, a final postponement decision, or broader customer consensus.

**Demand figures remain claims under discussion, not demonstrated recurring demand.** The retrieved consultation summary makes the distinction explicit:

> “In the fictional LoopLunch simulation, Maya Tan distinguishes stated reorder intentions from actual repeat purchasing: the quoted consultation reports 30 S$12 trial meals sold and 18 buyers expressing reorder intentions, without measured repeat purchases; 36 of 60 survey respondents would consider S$12 and 21 would consider S$14, but these voluntary intentions are not reservations.”

The searches did not establish that these trial transactions or survey responses occurred as executed events within this run. They should be treated as consultation-reported inputs, not added to the observed action count. Similarly, the reported surpluses of S$825 at S$12 and S$1,380 at S$14 depend on unproven forecasts of 45 and 32 daily meals respectively; they are not realized financial results.

**Operational proposals are not readiness evidence.** The retrieved material reports a trial punctuality figure of 26 out of 30 meals, but does not establish those deliveries as events executed during the recorded run. Subsequent retests remain proposals rather than completed demonstrations:

> “LoopLunch tells Daniel Koh that three successful 30-meal runs would not demonstrate readiness for the 45-meal S$12 forecast, and any approved cap should remain within demonstrated delivery and collection capability; dates, spending and launch remain unapproved.”

The supplier relationship and host arrangements are likewise described as prospective. Kitchen capacity is not reserved allocation, arrival before 12:15 p.m. is a requirement rather than a guarantee, and payment acknowledgments are not meal confirmations. The retrieved evidence leaves lobby authorization, backup coverage, payment sequencing and refund arrangements unresolved.

**The defensible outlook is therefore conditional and incomplete.** The supplied evidence does not establish final price approval, a confirmed launch, paid repeat purchasing, completed reliability retests or signed operational commitments. That is an evidence limit—not proof that these could never occur. The run supports identifying the conditions stakeholders requested before proceeding; it cannot establish sustained demand, operational readiness or the eventual choice between S$12, S$14 and postponement.

## Stakeholder Reactions: Accessibility Versus Reliability

**Stakeholders differed over price, but their reported positions shared a condition: affordability could not substitute for dependable service.** Within the early-stopped run, the retrieved exchanges show conditional negotiation rather than settled agreement. The three searches supplied attributed summaries, not authenticated verbatim posts or round-level provenance. Quotations below reproduce those summaries; they should not be read as direct transcripts. No direct employee or customer testimony was retrieved.

**The founder–operations disagreement was negotiable, not a fixed split.** Maya Tan’s accessibility preference left room for a higher price if paid commitments supported it. Daniel Koh acknowledged the lower price’s potential to encourage trial while favoring tighter operating constraints:

> “Daniel Koh addresses Maya Tan with a continued recommendation of S$14 for LoopLunch, one collection point, and a firm daily cap still to be set, while acknowledging that S$12 could encourage trial.”

The distinction was therefore one of emphasis: Maya prioritized accessible entry, while Daniel sought a more constrained service proposition. Neither position established that customers would accept the associated price or ordering arrangements. Their reported agreement that higher margins would not repair unreliable delivery limits any interpretation of S$14 as a solution in itself.

**The prospective host treated employee protection as a condition of promotion.** Aisha Lim’s concerns extended beyond whether meals arrived: employees needed enough certainty to arrange alternatives when an order remained unresolved.

> “Aisha Lim asks Daniel Koh to propose a deadline for confirming or declining each paid order and a notification process while supplier acceptance is pending so employees know when to arrange alternative lunches. She requests these details alongside support hours and the refund timetable in the draft employee notice, without presenting any as confirmed terms.”

This reaction frames reliability as usable information as well as delivery performance. Aisha also bounded the host’s role: facilitating access and sharing an offer did not mean guaranteeing purchases, subsidizing meals or absorbing customer-support duties. The retrieval reports a specific interaction supporting that boundary:

> “On Reddit, Aisha Lim likes Maya Tan’s comment assigning proposed weekend ordering support to LoopLunch rather than HarborWorks and requesting reviewed contact, backup, confirmation, cancellation/refund and payment-timing arrangements before employee commitments.”

That reported like supports a narrow reading of approval for the proposed responsibility allocation—not endorsement of the launch or evidence of employee demand.

**The supplier’s response centered on protecting production commitments, not choosing a retail price.** Ravi Menon’s position resisted treating expressions of interest as sufficient grounds to reserve production:

> “Ravi Menon requires confirmed LoopLunch volumes and a payment schedule before committing production at FreshBatch Kitchen. Changes to Ravi Menon's minimum-volume or payment position require a negotiated arrangement protecting production costs, rather than expressions of interest.”

The stakeholder tension was consequently broader than S$12 versus S$14. LoopLunch sought customer commitments, while its prospective supplier required separately agreed commercial protection. The retrieved exchanges do not show FreshBatch accepting the venture’s demand risk or assuming responsibility for transport. Its prospective status must not be mistaken for an operational commitment.

**Accessibility also concerned informed participation, not price alone.** Aisha asked for menu-specific evidence and private support before employees paid:

> “Aisha Lim asks Daniel Koh, together with Maya Tan, to obtain supplier-verified information for each proposed menu and identify a dietary-question contact before orders open. Employees should receive the information before prepaying, with clear menu-change notifications and options; office management cannot verify dietary suitability for the supplier, and individual dietary enquiries should remain off public threads.”

Maya’s and Daniel’s reported responses supported verification and private handling of dietary questions. FreshBatch, however, described the relevant menu information as unconfirmed. These exchanges demonstrate attention to participation barriers; they do not establish that either menu accommodated particular dietary needs.

**The missing customer voice remains decisive.** These are principally organizer, supplier and host perspectives on what employees would need. They cannot establish employees’ willingness to pay more for reliability, tolerate a previous-day cutoff or accept shared-lobby collection. Within the available evidence, support depended on explicit responsibilities, informed purchasing and credible service arrangements. It did not amount to customer consensus—or completed readiness—at either price.

## Demand, Cost and Capacity Risks

**The retrieved discussion identifies interacting demand, cost and capacity risks—not a demonstrated financial outcome.** Coverage ends at 102 recorded actions and round 37 of 336 planned rounds. The three searches supplied attributed graph summaries rather than authenticated agent transcripts or action-level provenance; quotations below reproduce those summaries. Financial figures remain scenario assumptions and modeled results, not realized receipts or losses.

**Weekday shortfalls matter more than a reassuring average.** The proposed procurement obligation applies separately to each weekday:

> “Ravi Menon clarifies for LoopLunch planning that FreshBatch Kitchen’s proposed minimum is 30 paid meals each weekday rather than a weekly average, so a stronger day cannot offset another day’s shortfall under the current proposal. Weekly payment in advance remains required, and supply arrangements remain prospective.”

The risk is therefore not simply whether enough people express interest overall, but whether paid commitments cover each day’s purchasing obligation. Neither price changes that proposed minimum. Moreover, 30 purchased meals is not an agreed customer-order cancellation threshold: the retrieved exchanges leave funding a shortfall or declining service as alternatives requiring a costed decision. No approved mechanism was established.

**S$14 provides more protection in the reported stress cases, but does not establish viability.** The equal-volume downside comparison is more informative about price protection than comparing base cases with different demand assumptions:

> “Daniel Koh reports that at 25 customer orders daily, LoopLunch's modeled six-week loss is S$825 at S$14 versus S$2,325 at S$12, including payment for the kitchen minimum.”

Supplier-cost sensitivity exposes a separate weakness:

> “FreshBatch Kitchen calls for explicit agreement on supplier-cost sensitivity before LoopLunch launches: under the brief's assumptions, a S$1 increase in food-and-packaging cost per meal turns the S$12 base case into a S$525 loss and leaves the S$14 base case with a S$420 surplus. Kitchen capacity does not establish delivery capacity or customer demand.”

The S$1 increase is explicitly a stress test, not a revised quotation. These examples support a narrower conclusion than recommending S$14 outright: that price fares better in the supplied calculations, conditional on their assumptions. They do not demonstrate that customers will purchase at S$14 or that its remaining surplus covers all unresolved obligations. The searches did not provide an itemized cost schedule sufficient to independently validate the models.

**The loss ceiling cannot be assessed using operating losses alone.** Retrieved entity summaries identify a S$10,000 reserve and S$3,000 loss ceiling as background planning inputs—not verified balances or spending authority. Maya’s reported position requires broader exposure to be considered:

> “Before committing, she requests written terms for the six-week price, weekly advance payments and cancellation obligations if service stops early, with downside assessment including refunds and unavoidable supplier commitments rather than only operating losses through the review date.”

The summaries also report her warning that the 25-order cases are not worst-case limits. Combined weak demand, higher costs and service setbacks remain unquantified. Retest spending is likewise unconfirmed, with quotations still needed for food, transport, backup coverage and handoff staffing. The supplied evidence consequently does not establish that total exposure would remain within the ceiling.

**Customer prepayment does not resolve supplier-payment timing.** The proposed Sunday customer cutoff is not an accepted kitchen deadline, and the kitchen’s exact advance-payment deadline remains unagreed:

> “FreshBatch Kitchen asks Daniel to propose a timetable distinguishing customer payment settlement, payment to FreshBatch Kitchen, order transmission and kitchen acceptance, including Monday orders. The sequence should be agreed before meal-confirmation deadlines are published or customer payments are taken.”

This identifies an unresolved funding dependency, not an observed cash shortfall. The record does not show that customer receipts would settle before supplier payment becomes due, or that refund funding has been secured. A proposed review after 10 service days would not itself remove supplier obligations or establish cancellation rights.

**Capacity limits must also constrain the financial forecast.** The retrieved operating proposal makes the relevant constraint explicit:

> “Daniel Koh recommends setting LoopLunch's firm daily cap according to confirmed kitchen, delivery and lobby handoff arrangements, prioritizing reliability before expansion rather than adopting FreshBatch's stated 70-meal daily capacity as the cap or treating it as reserved supply.”

The financial implication is that forecast sales volumes cannot be treated as achievable merely because the kitchen lists sufficient capacity. Any approved cap must be reconciled with the volume assumptions used to support the selected price. Neither an allocated production quantity nor a demonstrated end-to-end cap was established by the retrieved evidence.

**The resulting decision conditions are linked:** establish paid demand by weekday; verify costs at an operationally supported volume; agree how purchasing shortfalls are funded or service declined; and include advance payments, refunds and early-stop liabilities in combined downside testing. The recorded discussion supports postponement if those conditions remain unresolved—not a conclusion that either price has already passed them.

## Recommendation: Gate the Launch, Not Just the Price

**Withhold launch approval at either price until the required gates are evidenced.** This is a recommendation from the incomplete record, not a postponement decision made by the agents. The run stopped after 102 actions, reaching round 37 of 336 planned rounds; the three searches establish neither completed readiness checks nor final approval. Quotations below reproduce retrieved attributed summaries, not authenticated verbatim agent posts.

The clearest decision rule is service readiness before price selection:

> “In the fictional simulation, Maya Tan agrees with Aisha Lim that promotion should await credible reliability evidence and proposes retest success criteria of arrival before 12:15 p.m., meals ready for collection and an unobstructed lobby handoff. She would postpone at either price if service readiness remains unresolved on 23 October 2026.”

**Apply the gates in sequence—not as promises to resolve after launch.**

1. **Authorize the evidence-gathering first.** Daniel’s proposed retests require Maya’s spending review, supplier availability and host access. Agree beforehand how results affect the decision, rather than treating any successful demonstration as automatic approval.

> “LoopLunch asks Daniel Koh to specify how the proposed 19–21 October 2026 retest results would inform the 23 October 2026 decision, including consequences of failed or cancelled runs; criteria must be agreed before testing and every scheduled run must be reported, not just successes.”

Record complete-batch arrival separately from meal counts, collection readiness and lobby clearance. Test backup and missed-delivery notifications as proposed. Any initial cap should remain within demonstrated delivery and collection capability and confirmed supplier allocation. Three successful 30-meal runs would neither prove the proposed 95% reliability target nor validate the 45-meal S$12 forecast.

2. **Settle service and customer protections before requesting payment.** Require written production, transport, backup and handoff responsibilities; host authorization; and an agreed timetable connecting customer settlement, supplier advance payment and kitchen acceptance. Employee information should distinguish payment acknowledgment from meal confirmation and explain pending orders, confirmation deadlines, support coverage and funded refunds. Supplier-verified menu information, substitution handling and a private dietary contact must also precede prepayment.

> “Maya Tan says a review after 10 service days could help the fictional LoopLunch scenario but cannot replace launch checks. Maya Tan calls for cancellation and refund terms, including terms for postponement, to be confirmed before any prepayment request; those terms remain unresolved, and neither price nor launch is confirmed.”

This sequencing matters: collecting money to demonstrate demand must not bypass the safeguards stakeholders require before collection.

3. **Choose the price against paid weekday commitments and the approved operating scope.** Retain S$12 only if its actual commitments, checked costs and demonstrated service capacity support it. Consider S$14 if commitments at that price support a viable operation under the same gates. Do not substitute either price’s forecast volume for orders, or assume that a smaller operating cap preserves the original financial forecast.

> “Maya Tan favors S$12 for LoopLunch's accessibility, but would switch to S$14 if actual prepayments support that price or postpone if neither price secures viable commitments.”

The supplier minimum is not itself an approved customer-order threshold. Any decision to fund a weekday shortfall rather than decline service needs explicit costing and approval. The retrieved material supplies no settled price-specific demand threshold that would justify inventing a numerical pass mark here.

**Make the review an enforceable decision point, not an assumed escape route.** Before approval, Maya should receive the requested downside assessment covering combined demand, cost and service setbacks, refunds and unavoidable supplier commitments. The background loss ceiling should constrain that assessment, not function as a spending target. Pause conditions remain to be agreed; the searches do not establish approved numerical triggers.

> “Ravi Menon clarifies that LoopLunch's proposed review after 10 service days would neither change FreshBatch Kitchen's payment terms nor establish cancellation rights; these arrangements require explicit agreement before production is committed.”

Consequently, the proposed review cannot justify launching with unresolved obligations. Nor should retest success substitute for Aisha’s separate authorization of access and promotion.

**The conditional recommendation is therefore: approve only the price that passes every gate; otherwise postpone.** S$12 remains an accessibility option, while S$14 remains a financially conditional alternative—not a repair for unreliable service. On the evidence available at the stop, neither has earned launch approval. Whether subsequent commitments or tests would change that conclusion is unobserved.
